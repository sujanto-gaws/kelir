# Kelir User Manual

**Status:** Living · **Last updated:** 2026-10-01

How to use a running Kelir: raise a document, decide a task, find a supplier, and — for an administrator — configure the types, forms and roles that shape all three. It assumes somebody else has installed it. If nobody has, [Quick Start](02.%20Quick%20Start.md) gets a stack running in one sitting.

**This describes the screens that exist.** Where a capability is real over the API and has no screen, the manual says so in place rather than describing a page you would go looking for; §12 collects them. Where the interface behaves in a way that looks like a defect and is not — a refusal, a missing button, a file that will not download — the manual says why, because that is the question a manual is actually asked.

The vocabulary here is the product's own. Where a word carries more meaning than a screen can hold, the definition is in [concepts/01. Concept.md](../concepts/01.%20Concept.md) and the rules are in the [SRS](../requirements/srs.md).

---

## 1. The Five Words

Everything in Kelir is built from these, and nothing on a screen means anything without them.

| Word | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Document** | A business event — a purchase request, an onboarding, a change to a supplier. It happens once, moves through a controlled lifecycle, and is then history. Every transaction in Kelir is one |
| **Document type** | The configured shape of a kind of document: which form it renders, how it is numbered, which workflow routes it, what it is allowed to change. An administrator creates these; everybody else creates documents *from* them |
| **Form** | The fields, validation and calculations a document is filled in against, stored as a definition rather than written as code. A published form revision is frozen, and a document pins the exact revision it was filled against |
| **Workflow** | The route a submitted document takes — who approves it, in what order, under what conditions. While a workflow is running, it owns the document's status |
| **Task** | One person's or one role's piece of a running workflow: a decision waiting to be made. Tasks arrive in **My Tasks** |

Two further words appear on screens and are easy to confuse with each other:

- A **party** is anyone or anything the business deals with — a person or an organisation — and a **supplier**, **customer** or **employee** is a party holding that role. One record, many roles, which is why a supplier who becomes a customer does not become a second record.
- A **record status** is the governance lifecycle of a master-data record, which keeps existing. A **document status** belongs to an event, which does not. The two never appear on the same row.

## 2. Signing In

Go to the address your administrator gave you and sign in with your username and password.

**If the form asks for a tenant code**, the deployment runs in multi-tenant mode and your account lives in exactly one tenant. Your administrator knows the code; existing accounts on a deployment that has just turned the mode on all live in its default tenant. If the field is absent, the deployment is single-tenant and a tenant code would be ignored rather than obeyed.

**If you have forgotten your password**, use *Forgot your password* on the sign-in page. A link is emailed to you. On a development stack the mail is not sent anywhere — it lands in Mailpit, and the person running the stack can retrieve it.

**Changing your own password has no screen yet.** The endpoint exists and nothing calls it. Until it does, the route to a new password is *Forgot your password*, or an administrator resetting it for you. An account created by an administrator, and the first administrator created at install, are both flagged as needing a password change, and nothing in the interface acts on the flag — so treat it as a reminder to you rather than as something the product will enforce.

**Signing out** revokes the session. Changing a password revokes every refresh token on the account, so no session can be extended; an access token already issued stays valid until it expires, which is at most fifteen minutes after it was issued (§11.4).

## 3. Finding Your Way Around

The rail down the left is the navigation. **You see only what you may open** — an entry whose permission you do not hold is not drawn. This is cosmetic tidiness and nothing more: the server re-checks every request and is the only thing that decides, so a link you reach by typing its address still refuses you.

Built-in destinations, in the order they appear:

| Entry | What it is for | You need |
|---|---|---|
| **Dashboard** | The landing page: what is waiting for you, where your documents stand, and how long they take to be decided | a session to open it; `reporting:dashboard:read` for its cards |
| **My Tasks** | Decisions waiting for you | `workflow:task:read` |
| **Documents** | Everything you may read, and the way to raise a new one | `document:read` |
| **Notifications** | What has been sent to you | `notification:read` |
| **Master Data** | Parties, suppliers, customers, employees | `master-data:party:read` |
| **Users**, **Roles**, **Delegations**, **Tenants** | Administration (§11) | the matching `identity:` or `organization:` code |
| **Document Types**, **Forms**, **Lists**, **Navigation** | Configuration (§10) | the matching `document-type:` or `rad:` code |
| **External Systems** | The systems Kelir integrates with (§11.5) | `integration:external-system:read` |
| **Integration Logs** | Every call Kelir made to an external system, and what came back (§11.6) | `integration:log:read` |

**The dashboard is your own work at a glance.** It needs `reporting:dashboard:read`. Without it the page still opens — it is where everybody lands after signing in, so it cannot send you away — and says which permission you are missing. With it, the page shows seven cards:

| Card | Answers |
|---|---|
| **Waiting for you** | How many tasks are waiting for your decision, and how many of those are past their date |
| **Your drafts** | How many documents you raised and have not submitted |
| **Your pending tasks** | The top of your inbox, in the order **My Tasks** opens on, and how many more are waiting |
| **What is late** | Which of your waiting tasks are past their date, the longest late first |
| **What you touched last** | The documents you most recently worked on |
| **Your documents by status** | How many of the documents **you raised** are at each of the ten statuses (§4.5), drawn as a chart with the numbers under it |
| **How long approval takes** | For the documents **you raised** that were approved or rejected in the last 90 days: the median time, the slowest, and how many there were |

**A row opens its task or document only if you can open that screen too.** The dashboard's permission is not `workflow:task:read` or `document:read`, and a row is your own work either way, so without those the rows are still shown — as text rather than links.

**An empty card says so in words** — *You have not raised any documents yet* — so a card with nothing to show is never mistaken for one that failed to load. If the chart alone cannot be drawn, its card says that and keeps the numbers. **Whether a task is late is the server's answer**, not your browser's clock, so it is the same on every machine.

**An approval time runs from the first time you sent a document to its approval or rejection.** If an approver sent it back and you resubmitted it, the time it spent with you counts too — the card answers *how long did it take to get a decision*, not *how long did the last approver take*. A document still being approved, one that was withdrawn, and one decided more than 90 days ago are not counted.

**Your deployment may have added to the rail.** An administrator can configure extra entries pointing at configured lists (§10.3). These appear alongside the built-in ones. If the configured entries fail to load, the built-in navigation is still drawn — the sidebar is how you leave a page, so it does not disappear because a configuration call failed.

**Every list keeps its state in the address bar.** The page you are on, the text you searched for and the filters you set are all in the URL, so a filtered view can be bookmarked, linked to a colleague, and survives a reload. Searching happens when you submit, not as you type.

**Unlike a list, a chooser that picks one record from a long list searches as you type** ([#525](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/525)): the document type on **New document**, the form, list and workflow a document type binds, the roles on a user, the person and type of a delegation window, and the person a task is handed to. Each has a search box above it. It shows the first 100 records that match, searches as you pause typing (or at once on Enter), and says *Showing 100 of N — refine your search to find the rest* when there are more. It matches the record's code or key and its name — for a person, the username, email or display name. A choice already made stays shown by its name while you search for something else. Choosers over a short fixed list, such as a status, do not search.

## 4. Documents

### 4.1 Finding one

**Documents** lists what you may read, with search and filters. A document has two identifiers and they are not the same thing:

- The **reference** (`DOC-2026-000123`) is the internal handle. A document has one from the moment it is created.
- The **number** is the business number, assigned by the numbering rule **when the document is submitted**. A draft has none. If you are looking for a document by a number somebody quoted at you and finding nothing, the likeliest reason is that it has not been submitted.

### 4.2 Raising one

**Documents → New**. Choose a type, give the document a title, and it opens. If your tenant has many types, type part of its name or code into the search box above them (§3).

Only **active** types are offered. If the type you expect is missing, it is still a draft, or it has been deprecated to stop new documents being raised from it while the existing ones carry on. A type that has no form bound to it is shown and cannot be started from, and the screen says which — that state is ordinary, because a type is often configured before its form exists.

### 4.3 The workspace

A document opens on six tabs.

| Tab | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Form** | The fields, and the buttons that move the document |
| **History** | This document's **status changes** — one row per transition, with who and why |
| **Workflow** | The running process: where it is, who has it, and what has been decided |
| **Attachments** | Files and links (§5) |
| **Comments** | The conversation (§6) |
| **Activity** | Everything that has happened to the document, from all four sources |

**History and Activity are one word apart and answer different questions.** History is the status trail and nothing else. Activity is the whole timeline — lifecycle, workflow, attachments and comments in one list, each entry labelled with where it came from. When you want *how did this get here*, read History; when you want *what has been done to this*, read Activity.

An Activity entry does not name its subject — no file name, no comment text, no second party to a delegation. Those live behind their own permissions, and the entry links to them rather than repeating them. So a row reads *a file was attached, by Sara, at 09:05*, and the file's name is on the Attachments tab, where the permission to see it is decided.

### 4.4 Filling it in, and submitting

**A document can be edited while it is a draft, and while it has been returned to you.** Anything else is read-only, and the fields are drawn disabled rather than hidden — you can read what was submitted.

**Save** keeps your work. **Submit** sends it. Submitting does three things at once: it validates the whole payload **on the server**, assigns the business number, and moves the document out of `Draft`.

**The validation that counts is the server's.** The form checks what it can as you type, and that is a convenience; the answer is the one the submit returns. A refusal lands where it belongs — a missing required field lands on the field, and a refusal about the type's configuration lands as a message naming what an administrator has to fix. Neither is flattened into *something went wrong*.

**A submit refused because the type has no numbering rule is not your problem to solve.** It means the type was configured without deciding how its documents are numbered. Send the message to whoever administers document types; §10.5 is what they need to do.

**A returned document is corrected and submitted again, and keeps the number it already has.** That is the whole difference between a return and a rejection: a rejection ends the document, a return asks you to fix it.

### 4.5 Statuses, and who moves them

| Status | Means |
|---|---|
| `Draft` | Being written. Editable. Not yet numbered |
| `Submitted` | Sent, not yet being decided |
| `In review` | Being looked at |
| `Pending approval` | **A workflow is deciding it** |
| `Approved` / `Rejected` | Decided |
| `Returned` | Sent back for correction. Editable again, number kept |
| `Completed` | Finished after approval |
| `Cancelled` | Stopped |
| `Archived` | Put away |

**When a workflow is running, the workspace's transition buttons are gone and that is correct.** A running process owns the document's status, and the way the document moves is through a task in **My Tasks** — not through a button here. A status set here would disagree with the process the moment it moved, so the product refuses it rather than allowing two things to decide one field.

**When no workflow is bound to the type**, the workspace offers the moves the server will accept from where the document is, and you move it by hand. This is the simpler configuration and a perfectly legitimate one; it is also what a newly configured deployment does before anyone has written a workflow definition.

## 5. Attachments and Links

The **Attachments** tab holds two different things, deliberately together: files stored by Kelir, and links to somewhere else. A person looking for the quotation does not care which it arrived as, so they are in one list — but they must not be mistaken for one another, so a link carries a **Link** badge, has no size and no scan status, and opens outward instead of downloading.

**A newly uploaded file cannot be downloaded immediately, and this is a security control rather than a delay.** Every upload is virus-scanned, and until the scan clears it the file is refused. Three refusals look similar and mean different things:

| State | Means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| `PENDING` | Not scanned yet | Wait. The scanner looks every few seconds |
| `INFECTED` | The scanner found something | Nothing. The file will not be released |
| `FAILED` | The scan could not run | **Not a Kelir error to shrug at.** A scan that did not run has cleared nothing, so the file is refused exactly as an infected one is. Re-upload it; if it keeps failing, tell whoever runs the deployment |

There are limits an administrator sets: a maximum size (25 MB by default) and a list of accepted file types (PDF, PNG, JPEG, and Word and Excel documents by default). **The type is detected from the file's contents, not from its name**, so renaming a file to get past the list does not work.

Deleting an attachment removes it from the document; the stored bytes are a retention matter and are not destroyed by the delete.

## 6. Comments

The **Comments** tab is a conversation about the document. Write one, reply to one, edit your own, delete your own.

**A comment is not a decision reason.** The Workflow tab one panel across shows decisions *with the reasons they were given*, and those look exactly like comments with authors. They are not the same thing and they are kept in different tabs on purpose: a decision's reason is written with the approval, is part of the record of that approval, and can never be edited or deleted. A comment can be all three. The note under the comment box says so, for the same reason this paragraph does.

**A reply sits under what it answers, and threads are one level deep.** You reply to a comment; you do not reply to a reply. If a root comment is on another page of a long conversation, its reply is still shown rather than dropped.

**A deleted comment that has replies leaves a marker** — *this comment was deleted*, with its author and time and nothing else. The replies under it are somebody else's words and are not taken away with it.

**An edited comment says that it was edited**, beside its time.

## 7. Tasks

### 7.1 The inbox

**My Tasks** is what is waiting for you: tasks assigned to you, and tasks offered to a role you hold. Nothing is filtered in your browser — the rule is in the server's own query, because showing you a task the server would then refuse you reads as a broken product while being a leak.

**Yours and unclaimed are shown apart, because they are different situations.** A task assigned to you is work you have. An unclaimed one is work going spare, and somebody else may take it while you are reading.

Four scopes:

| Scope | Shows |
|---|---|
| **Waiting for me** | Open tasks |
| **Late** | Open tasks past their due date — a narrowing of the above, not a separate axis |
| **Decided by me** | What you have already decided |
| **Everything I have held** | Both |

### 7.2 Deciding one

Open a task. If it is unclaimed, **claim** it first — that is what stops two people deciding the same thing.

**The decisions on offer come from the workflow, not from a fixed list.** A definition decides what its tasks may do, so one task offers *Approve* and *Reject* and another adds *Return*. A button you do not see is one this task does not have, not one you are forbidden.

**Some decisions require a reason and will not proceed without one.** The reason is entered *with* the decision rather than after it — a screen that recorded the decision and then asked why would have recorded a decision with no reason attached the moment somebody closed it. What you write becomes part of the record of that approval, permanently, and is not a comment (§6).

**Returning sends the document back to its author to correct**, keeping its number. Rejecting ends it.

### 7.3 Handing a task over

**Delegate** hands one task to a named person. That is a single handover, decided by you, now. The person is found by searching their name, username or email (§3); you are not offered, and neither is anybody who cannot sign in.

For planned absence, use a **delegation window** instead (§11.3) — that routes work to somebody else for a period, rather than moving one task.

## 8. Notifications

**Notifications** is what has been sent to you: a record of what reached you, not a live to-do list.

The difference matters. When a task is offered to a role, every holder of that role is notified, and the moment one of them claims it the others are holding a notification about something no longer waiting for them. **Those rows stay** — they were true when they were written, and deleting them would take away the answer to *what was I told* from somebody who is reading it. **My Tasks is the live view**, and every task notification links to it.

Following a notification marks it read. There is also an explicit *mark read*, for the case where you can tell from the list that you do not need to open it.

**A notification may arrive by email as well**, if the deployment is configured for it. Email is sent once per channel and a failure is logged rather than retried — so the notification centre, not your inbox, is the reliable record.

## 9. Master Data

**Master Data** has four views over one set of records:

| View | Shows |
|---|---|
| **Parties** | Everyone and everything the business deals with, whatever role they hold |
| **Suppliers** | Parties holding the supplier role, with the supplier number that makes them one |
| **Customers** | The same, with customer numbers |
| **Employees** | The same, with employee numbers |

**One party, many roles.** A supplier who becomes a customer gains a role; they do not become a second record. This is why the Parties view exists beside the three role views.

**The three role views can be searched and filtered; the Parties view cannot**, and it offers no search box rather than offering one that would be ignored.

**You may see some tabs and not others.** The role views need the permission to read role assignments as well as the permission to read parties, because a row is a party with a supplier number on it and is made of both. If you hold one permission and not the other, you get the tabs you may open rather than a screen that refuses all four.

**In this release the master-data screens are read-only.** Creating and editing party records is real over the API and has no screen. There is a deliberate route to changing governed master data that does not need one, and it is the whole point of the design: **a change to a supplier is raised as a document**. An administrator configures a document type whose target is a party, somebody raises it, it goes through approval like anything else, and the record changes when the document is approved. A record awaiting that approval sits at `Pending approval` — the governance lifecycle in §1, not a document status.

**A business code is never reused.** A supplier number belongs to that supplier permanently, even if the record is deleted; the next supplier gets the next number. If you are looking for a gap in a numbering sequence, that is where it came from.

## 10. Configuration

These screens belong to whoever configures the deployment. Everything here shapes what §4 to §9 look like for everybody else.

### 10.1 Forms

**Forms** lists form definitions; opening one is the builder. A form is a definition — components, validation rules, conditional logic and calculations — not code, and it is edited here rather than deployed.

**A form has revisions, and publishing freezes one.** A published revision cannot be edited: a document pins the exact revision it was filled against and re-renders against that revision for ever, so editing a published one would change what an old document says it recorded. To change a published form, create a new revision.

**Only a published revision can be bound to a document type**, which is the most common reason a form does not appear in the type's chooser.

**Publishing re-validates the whole definition**, so a publish can be refused for something a save allowed. That is deliberate: a rule the engine could not run — a calculation that depends on itself, a condition referring to a field that is not there — is caught here rather than in front of the person filling the form in. A refusal names what is wrong.

### 10.2 Lists

**Lists** configures the list screens people use: which columns, which filters, which sort. A list is a definition too, and adding one needs a definition rather than a code change. A configured list is reachable at its own address and can be pointed to from the navigation (§10.3).

**Opening a configured list needs the permission of the rows behind it**, not the permission to build lists. Otherwise only a configuration administrator could open a screen built for everybody.

### 10.3 Navigation

**Navigation** configures the extra entries in the left-hand rail — typically pointing at the lists from §10.2. Built-in entries are not configured here and cannot be removed.

### 10.4 Document types

**Document Types** is where a kind of document is defined. A type carries:

| Setting | What it decides |
|---|---|
| **Code** and **name** | What it is called |
| **Status** | `Draft` while being set up, `Active` to let people raise documents from it, `Deprecated` to stop new ones while existing documents carry on |
| **Form** | Which published form its documents are filled against |
| **List** | Which configured list its documents appear on |
| **Workflow** | Which active workflow routes it. Leave it empty and documents are moved by hand (§4.5) |
| **Target** | Whether this type exists to change a master-data record (§9), and which kind |
| **Security level** | How the document is classified |

**The bindings are chosen from what can actually be bound** — published forms, active lists, active workflows — rather than typed, so a broken binding is refused before it is saved rather than discovered when somebody raises a document. Each chooser searches by key and title or name (§3), so a form or workflow is reachable however many others the tenant holds.

**Deleting a type is refused while documents exist from it.** `Deprecated` is the control for retiring one.

### 10.5 Numbering

Each type has its own **Numbering** rule, and a type with no rule is an ordinary state rather than an error — the numbering is usually decided after the type exists. **But a submit against a type with no rule is refused**, so the rule has to exist before anybody uses the type in anger.

A rule is a template, a scope and a padding:

- **Template** — `PO-{year}-{sequence}`. The placeholders are `{sequence}`, `{year}`, `{month}` and `{department}`. **`{sequence}` is required**: without it every document of the type would be given the same number, and the second one would be refused at submit having already done the work. An unknown placeholder is left in the output rather than blanked, so a mistake in a template is visible as a mistake.
- **Scope** — when the sequence restarts: never (`Global`), each calendar year, each calendar month, or per department per year.
- **Padding** — how many digits the sequence is padded to.
- **Gap policy** — whether a failed submission gives its number back (`Gapless`) or leaves a hole (`Allow gaps`). Choose `Gapless` where an auditor will ask about missing numbers.

### 10.6 Workflows

**There is no workflow builder screen.** Forms, lists, navigation and document types each have one; workflow definitions are written and published over the API, against the [JSON Workflow Schema](../schema/JSON%20Workflow%20Schema.md). Until a screen exists, a deployment that wants approval routing needs somebody who can call the API — and a deployment that does not want it binds no workflow and moves documents by hand (§4.5), which is a supported configuration rather than a workaround.

**A definition the engine could never run is refused when it is saved**, not when a document reaches the broken part of it. So a definition that saved and published is one the engine has already agreed it can execute.

Only an **active** definition can be bound to a document type, the same rule forms follow.

## 11. Administration

### 11.1 Users

**Users** creates and edits accounts and assigns roles to them. The role list in the dialog searches by role code and name (§3); the roles an account already holds stay ticked whatever you search for, and without `identity:role:read` the dialog shows no role list and leaves the account's roles as they are. A username cannot be changed after the account is created; an email can. A duplicate username or email is refused by the server and shown against the field that caused it.

An administrator can reset another account's password. A person cannot yet change their own from within the product (§2).

**There are two ways to stop an account, and they do not act at the same speed.**

- **Deactivate, on the user list, removes the account and signs that person out at once.** The account leaves the list and cannot sign in, and the next thing that person does in Kelir sends them to the sign-in page. You cannot deactivate your own account.
- **Changing an account's status to `Inactive` in the edit dialog keeps the account on the list and leaves the person signed in for up to fifteen minutes.** They cannot sign in again and their session cannot be extended, but somebody already signed in carries on until their access token expires (§11.4).

Use Deactivate when the person must be out now.

**Taking a role away from somebody is not refused because of open work.** Removing somebody's last grant of a role, or deactivating or deleting their account, can leave that role's open tasks with nobody to decide them. §11.2 says how those tasks are found and cleared.

### 11.2 Roles and permissions

**Roles** creates roles and chooses the permissions each grants. A permission is a code of the form `module:resource:action` — `document:create`, `master-data:party:read` — and the catalogue is grouped by module in the dialog, because a hundred ungrouped checkboxes is not a list anybody can read. A role needs a code and a name: the code is at most 64 characters and the name at most 200, spaces at either end not counted. A code cannot be changed after the role is created, and a duplicate code is refused. The server refuses a blank or over-long field and shows why against that field ([#575](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/575)).

**Permissions are granted through roles, never to a person directly.** To give somebody an ability, give them a role that grants it.

**A role that open work still needs cannot be deleted.** Deleting it is refused, with the number of open tasks that need it, and nothing changes. **The refusal lists those tasks** on the Roles page, in place of the confirmation: each with its task reference, its document's number and title, the state its workflow is in, who holds it if anyone has claimed it, and why it needs the role: offered to it and unclaimed, a decision reserved to it, or both ([#532](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/532)). A task needs a role when it is offered to that role **and nobody has claimed it yet**, or when a decision on it is reserved to that role by the workflow, claimed or not. A claimed task does not need the role it was offered to: the person who claimed it can still decide it. Have those tasks decided or reassigned, and that reason goes away. **A task is reassigned from that list** ([#512](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/512)): an administrator who also holds `workflow:task:reassign` sees **Reassign** on each task, and nobody else does. It opens a dialog: choose **A role** or **A user**, never both, then the role or the user from the list, add an optional reason (kept in the task's history), and press **Reassign**. A user then holds the task; a role's holders are offered it unclaimed. The role being deleted is not in the list, and neither is a user who cannot sign in. The task keeps its place in the workflow. A reassign is refused to somebody who could not decide the task, under the role or user field with the reason, which names what the task's decisions need: a user must be allowed to take at least one of its decisions, and a role must be one the workflow names for them. A user or role deleted meanwhile is refused the same way, and a task decided meanwhile is refused with a message on the dialog. After a reassign the list is read again; once it is empty, it says the delete can be tried again. A reassign clears the way for a task offered to the role; a task whose decision the workflow reserves to the role still needs the role until it is decided, and a reassign moves it to somebody who can decide it. Nothing cancels a task. The seeded administrator role cannot be deleted at all.

**Nor can a role that a published workflow names.** A workflow names a role when it offers a step's task to that role, or reserves a decision to it. Deleting the role would stop every document routed to that workflow at the step that needs it, so the delete is refused, and the refusal lists each workflow by its key, name and revision. **You are told one reason at a time**: while open tasks need the role, that is the refusal you see, and the workflows are named only once no open task needs it. What counts:

- a **published** revision always counts;
- a **deprecated** revision counts while approvals are still running on it, because they can still reach the step that names the role;
- a **draft** does not count, and nor does a step's escalation, which nothing acts on yet.

**Publishing a new revision does not retire the old one.** The earlier revision stays published, and nothing in the product deprecates one yet ([#573](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/573)). So an old published revision keeps the role held until it is removed, and publishing a revision that no longer names the role is not enough on its own. To free the role:

1. Publish a revision that does not name the role.
2. Bind the document types that used the old revision to the new one.
3. Delete the old revision. That is refused while approvals are still running on it, so have them finished first.

The delete of the role then goes through. It does not stop somebody publishing a workflow that names the role afterwards: publishing does not yet check that the roles a workflow names exist ([#572](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/572)).

**A role that is not deleted can still be left with open tasks and nobody to decide them** ([#508](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/508)). This happens when its last holder leaves it, in any of three ways:

- their last grant of the role is removed on the user's edit screen (§11.1);
- the end date of their grant passes;
- their account is deactivated or deleted.

Unlike the delete above, none of these is refused. The tasks stay open and their documents stay at `Pending approval`. **The Roles page marks such a role** with its count of open tasks and zero active holders. Only an administrator who holds `workflow:task:reassign` sees the mark. It links to the role's list of open tasks, the same list a refused delete shows, which needs `identity:role:delete` to open. From there, **Reassign** each task to a user or a role that can decide it, as above. The counts are live. A holder counts only while their grant is within its dates and their account is active and not deleted, so the mark goes once no open task needs the role or somebody holds it again. **Kelir never reassigns these tasks for you.**

**Hiding a navigation entry is not a security control.** The server checks every request against the permissions your session actually carries, and refuses regardless of what your browser drew. Equally, a screen may be open to you while a button on it is refused — reading a task and deciding it are different permissions, and the product shows you the task you were told about rather than hiding it because you may not decide it.

### 11.3 Delegations

A **delegation window** routes work to somebody else for a period — cover for leave, in practice.

**A window is opened and ended. It is never edited.** Editing one in place would change where work was being routed with nothing on the record saying it had changed; ending one and opening another leaves both facts visible.

**You open a window in your own name and nobody else's.** There is no field for whose authority is being handed over — a person who could name somebody else could point the finance director's approvals at themselves, and the row would look exactly like legitimate cover. Reading and *ending* windows are administrative, because the row somebody has to be able to find is the one whose owner went on leave without ending it.

**Ending a window stops work arriving; it does not reach back.** Tasks already handed over stay where they are — moving approvals out from under somebody mid-decision, on a schedule nobody triggered, is what both halves of that rule avoid. The same is true opening one: it routes what comes next, not what is already assigned.

**Two columns, because they are two facts.** A window ends either by being switched off or by its end date passing, and the list shows both — cover that was cancelled is not the same as cover that ran its course.

### 11.4 Tenants

Only on a deployment running in multi-tenant mode.

**Tenants are administered only from the deployment's default tenant.** Holding the permission is not enough, and a tenant's own administrator is never granted it — otherwise a tenant administrator could create tenants and grant themselves any permission in the catalogue. If you hold the permission and are refused, you are signed in to the wrong tenant.

**Do not create tenants on a single-tenant deployment.** Sign-in resolves the default tenant and ignores the code, so the new tenant's administrator is a real account that nobody can reach.

**Suspending or deactivating a tenant stops its users signing in. It does not sign out somebody who is already signed in, not straight away.** From the moment you save the change, nobody in that tenant can sign in and no session of theirs can be extended. Somebody who was already signed in can carry on working until their access token expires, which is at most fifteen minutes after it was issued. After that they are sent to the sign-in page and refused there. If you need to be sure that nobody in the tenant is still working, wait fifteen minutes after you suspend it. `Suspended` and `Inactive` do the same thing. Changing one person's account status to `Inactive` in the edit dialog has the same limit; the Deactivate button on the user list does not, because it removes the account and signs that person out at once (§11.1). The time is fixed. It is not a setting.

**Deleting a tenant signs everybody in it out at once.** From the moment the tenant is deleted, nobody in it can sign in, no session can be extended, and the next thing anybody already signed in does is refused and sends them to the sign-in page. Nothing more can be read or changed in the tenant. There is no need to suspend it first.

### 11.5 External systems

**External Systems** lists the systems Kelir integrates with, such as an ERP, an HR system or an e-signature provider. You can search by code or name and filter by status and type. Opening a system shows its settings, its **endpoints** and its **credential references**.

**This screen records configuration, and the only call it makes is a test call you ask for.** Registering a system records its code, name, type, base URL, authentication type, timeout and retry policy for the integrations that will use it. No integration uses them yet. *Test call*, below, calls one endpoint once so you can see that the settings work.

**A system is never deleted.** *Deactivate* takes it out of service and keeps it on the list, and *Activate* puts it back. Both buttons need `integration:external-system:deactivate`, and they are the only way into or out of `Inactive`. The *Edit* dialog does not offer that status. *Edit* can move a system between `Active` and `Maintenance`, which means the system is being worked on, not that it is out of service. A system's code cannot be changed after it is registered.

**Endpoints are part of the system.** You can see them if you can see the system, and you can add or edit them if you can edit the system. An endpoint is not deleted either. Set it to `Inactive` to retire it. An endpoint's path may carry a query, such as `/orders/{id}/lines?expand=true`. **That query is configuration, and Kelir does not protect it**: the path is shown to everyone who can read the system and written to the audit trail, as you typed it. Never put a key or token in a path. Record a credential reference instead.

**A credential is a reference to where a secret is kept.** Put the secret in the environment or the vault first. Then enter where it is: `env://KELIR_INTEGRATION_SECRET_SYSTEM__ERP_API_KEY` for an environment variable, or `vault://kelir/erp/api-key` for a vault path, optionally with `#field`. **A test call reads only an `env://` reference, and only a variable named for your tenant**: `KELIR_INTEGRATION_SECRET_`, your tenant code in upper case with any `-` written as `_`, two underscores, then the name, which is upper-case letters, digits and underscores. Tenant `TNT-001` uses `KELIR_INTEGRATION_SECRET_TNT_001__ERP_API_KEY`. A name that another tenant's code also covers is refused for both tenants, so `A-B` and `A_B`, which are both written `A_B`, can read neither's variables. Any other name, and every `vault://` reference, is saved but refused when called. Setting the variable is for whoever runs Kelir ([Installation and Deployment](01.%20Installation%20and%20Deployment.md) §7.1). **Enter the location, never the secret itself, not even as part of a path.** What you enter is stored as you typed it, shown to everyone who can read credential references, and written to the audit trail. Kelir checks only that the reference has one of these two forms. It does not check that the variable or path exists. For the same reason, do not put a user name or password in a base URL. Record a credential reference instead. **A base URL is a scheme, host, port and path, and a query string is refused** (`QUERY_IN_BASE_URL`), because the URL is shown to everyone who can read the system and is written to the audit trail.

**A test call sends one real request to one endpoint, and shows you the answer.** On the system's page, each active endpoint has a *Test call* button, shown only if you hold `integration:endpoint:call`. That permission is separate from editing the system, so you can hold either without the other. A retired endpoint has no button. The dialog says what will be sent and asks you to confirm. Kelir then sends the endpoint's method, with no body, to the base URL joined with the endpoint's path, using the system's one credential reference that is active and valid today. Only *Bearer token* and *Basic auth* credentials can be sent. For Basic auth the secret is `user:password`. **Any method other than GET is a real request that the system may act on**, and the dialog says so before you confirm.

The call runs while you wait, for up to the system's timeout. Redirects are not followed. **Every call is logged, including one that is refused**, and the dialog shows the log id. If you hold `integration:log:read`, the id is a link that opens the record in **Integration Logs** (§11.6). There are four kinds of outcome:

| Outcome | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Success** and an HTTP code | The system answered with a 2xx. The dialog shows the code, how long the call took, the method and URL, and the start of the response body. |
| **Failed** and an HTTP code | The system answered, but not with a 2xx: a 4xx or 5xx, or a 3xx because redirects are not followed. The call worked. The answer is what needs attention. |
| **Refused before anything was sent** | A setting is wrong, and nothing left Kelir. The dialog names the problem and says what to fix. The common problems are a system or endpoint that is not active, no base URL, no credential that is active and valid today or more than one, a credential type that cannot be sent, and a secret that cannot be read, such as a variable outside your tenant's `KELIR_INTEGRATION_SECRET_<CODE>__`, a name with a character other than an upper-case letter, a digit or an underscore, a name another tenant's code also covers, a variable that is not set, or a `vault://` reference. The host may also not resolve, or may resolve to an address Kelir will not connect to. A private network address is allowed only if whoever runs Kelir lists its range. Loopback, link-local and cloud metadata addresses never are. |
| **The system did not answer** | Kelir tried and got nothing back. The connection failed or was closed, or the system did not answer within its timeout. Check that the system is running and reachable from the Kelir server, or raise the timeout. |

**The body shown is the start of the response only.** It is at most 2,048 characters, and the dialog says when there was more. ~~Any secret Kelir sent is masked wherever it appears in the body, and no headers are shown.~~ No headers are shown. If the system echoes the secret Kelir sent, the echo is redacted in the spellings Kelir lists: as sent, as its `Authorization` header, and base64, percent-encoded or `\u`-escaped. A spelling outside that list, such as a double percent-encoding, is shown exactly as the system sent it (struck 2026-10-01, [#621](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/621)).

**The credential section is hidden unless you hold `integration:credential:read`.** Seeing a system does not let you see where its secrets are kept, so a missing section means you lack that permission, not that the system has no credentials. Adding, editing and removing a reference each need their own `integration:credential:` permission.

**Who starts with these permissions.** The system tenant's administrator starts with all of them. In a multi-tenant deployment:

- **A tenant created after the upgrade that added this screen**: its administrator starts with the `integration:external-system:` permissions, so the entry appears, and **not** with the `integration:credential:` ones. Seeing where secrets are kept is a grant somebody makes deliberately, through a role (§11.2).
- **A tenant created before that upgrade**: its administrator starts with none of them, and the entry does not appear until they are granted through a role.
- **`integration:endpoint:call`, which test calls need**, came in a later upgrade. The administrator of a tenant created after it starts with the permission. The administrator of a tenant created before it does not, and needs it granted through a role.

### 11.6 Integration logs

**Integration Logs** lists every call Kelir made to an external system, newest first. Today that means the test calls of §11.5, because nothing else calls out yet. Each row shows when the call started, the system, the method and URL, the outcome and how long it took. A failed call shows its error under the URL.

**You need `integration:log:read`, and it is separate from the registry's permissions.** Reading the log does not let you manage systems, and managing systems does not show you the log, because a log row holds what was sent and received. The system tenant's administrator starts with it. In a multi-tenant deployment, the administrator of a tenant created after the upgrade that added this screen starts with it too. The administrator of a tenant created before that upgrade does not, and needs it granted through a role (§11.2).

**Filter by system, status and start time.** *System* lists the registered systems. It needs `integration:external-system:read` as well; without it the chooser is off and says so. *Started from* includes the moment you choose, and *Started before* excludes it, so two ranges that meet do not share a call. The times are in your browser's time zone. Like every list, the filters live in the address bar (§3).

| Status | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Success** | The system answered with a 2xx |
| **Failed** | The system answered with anything else, or the call was refused before anything was sent, or it got no answer. The error says which |
| **Pending**, **Retrying**, **Dead letter** | For calls that are retried. Nothing retries yet (§12), so no row has these |

**Details opens the whole record** in a dialog: the status and HTTP code, the error, the system, the direction and type, the method and URL, what the call was about (a test call names the endpoint), the correlation id, and when it started and finished. Below them are the **request and response payloads**, laid out as JSON. A call that was refused or got no answer has no response, and the dialog says so. The dialog's address can be shared: it is the list's address with `?log=` and the record's id.

**The payloads are shown exactly as they were stored.** ~~Any secret Kelir sent was masked before the record was written, so an `Authorization` header reads `[REDACTED]`. Nothing in Kelir can show the unmasked value.~~ The `Authorization` header Kelir sent reads `[REDACTED]`. In the response, an echo of the secret is redacted in the spellings §11.5 lists, and a spelling outside that list is stored and shown exactly as the system sent it, to everyone who can read the log (struck 2026-10-01, [#621](https://github.com/sujanto-gaws/kelir/issues/621)). A credential is named by its id and type, never by its reference.

**Links appear only where you may follow them.** The system links to its page if you hold `integration:external-system:read`. A record made for a document links to it if you hold `document:read`. Without the permission, the value is shown as text. No test call is made for a document, so that link does not appear yet.

**The log cannot be edited or deleted** from the screen or the API.
