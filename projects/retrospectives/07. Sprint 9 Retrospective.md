## What Worked

- **The construction plan took eight decisions before the first commit, and seven of them survived.** §4.2's draft strictness, §4.4's audit boundary, §6.2's legality table, §7.1's delegated permission, §8.1's visibility rule — each was written down before the code and none had to be re-argued mid-item. The one that moved is §4.2's own table, which gained a third forgiven class during construction; the plan records that where it was wrong rather than in a footnote.
- **Dating the mid-sprint pass worked, and it worked *because* it was a date.** The [Sprint 8 retrospective](06.%20Sprint%208%20Retrospective.md)'s first action; a position between two items cannot be missed visibly and a date can. It ran on 2026-08-28 and produced three findings, all closed inside the sprint — against Sprint 7's seven filed and none closed.
- **The mutation campaign paid for itself twice over, and neither payment was the ratio.** It found a flaky test that six clean runs had not (F1), and the discipline of reading its greens one at a time found five predicates that were the only guard on their behaviour (F3). The number it produces is the least valuable thing it does.
- **Taking #218 first was right.** The construction plan put it before item 1 for the same reason Sprint 8 put #200 there, and its fixture rule — *a scoping test puts a second subject in the database* — is what made F3's five tests obvious to write when the campaign surfaced them.
- **The browser flow found two defects in itself on its first CI run**, both the spec's rather than the product's, and both the kind only a real deployment produces.

## What Didn't

- **The same defect was written twice, twelve lines apart, and the first fix did not find the second.** F2. `numbering_service::allocate` took a second pooled connection in its policy *read*; a concurrency test went red; the fix was aimed at what the test had shown. The allocation itself was doing the same thing in the same function and survived, because nobody re-read the path against [coding standard](../../docs/standards/01.%20Coding%20Standard.md) §2.5 — the rule that had just been broken. It is the release-blocking finding of a phase close and it was found by reading, not by testing.
- **A flaky test was written, run six times green, and cited in a commit message as evidence.** F1. It asserted one refusal code where the product legitimately answers two, and only load made the difference visible. The commit that introduced it claimed it held the compare-and-swap; it did not, and a second test had to be written to actually hold it.
- **The first campaign's number was wrong and looked right.** 30% — a plausible figure, above the previous sprint's, and entirely an artefact of the flake. Had the greens not been read individually, it would have been reported.
- **The release rehearsal ran at the close, not at the entry.** The [Sprint 8 retrospective](06.%20Sprint%208%20Retrospective.md) made it an action, the sprint plan made it an entry criterion, the construction plan §10 listed it as a dated step — and construction still ran first, because nothing in the sprint's sequence *needed* it. A rehearsal produces no artefact the next item consumes.
- **No item is `Done`.** There was no second reader, so §2's rule applies to the whole sprint at once. This is the second time a sprint has closed with every row `author-verified` and the first time it has been true of the construction as well as the fixes.

## Root Causes

- **A fix aimed at a symptom stops where the symptom stopped.** F2's first half was found by a red test, so the fix was scoped to what turned that test green. The rule it violated covers the whole function, and nothing prompted a re-read of the whole function. **The test that exposes a rule violation defines the symptom, not the scope.**
- **A concurrency test asserts the outcome the author imagined.** F1's author knew about the 409 path and wrote it down; the 422 path — a caller that reads the *new* status and is refused as an illegal move — is equally correct, was not imagined, and appears only when scheduling delays a caller past the winner's commit. Six green runs is not evidence about a race; it is six samples of one interleaving.
- **An entry criterion with no consumer is advisory.** Every criterion this sprint met was one whose output something downstream needed: #218's tests fed the campaign, the gap policy fed item 2, the dated pass fed the findings. The rehearsal fed nothing, so it drifted to the end. **Sequencing is what makes a criterion binding; dating is only what makes missing it visible.**
- **One author cannot supply the defects nobody thought to look for.** Record 06 §1 says this at length. The three findings this sprint produced were all *refutations of its own claims*, which is the one category a self-pass can reach — and it reached them only because a machine (the campaign) and a rule (§2.5) were pointed at the work rather than a person.

## The phase, in one paragraph

Phase 4 took three sprints because **D-16** counted it again and added one, and the count was right: Sprint 7 built the metadata and the harness, Sprint 8 built the renderer and the server that does not trust it, and Sprint 9 built the document those two existed for. The phase ships its whole `Must` scope and none of its `Should` scope, which is what D-16 accepted at the time. What it does not ship is an independently verified Sprint 9 — the phase's last sprint verified itself, and the record says so.
