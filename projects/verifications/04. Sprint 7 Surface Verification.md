### F1 — `DEPARTMENT_YEAR` re-issues `000001` forever

Database Schema §6.3 states the storage rule and its reason in one sentence:

> **One bucket per rule.** `sequence_key` holds the bucket the counter is in, so a document back-dated into a bucket the sequence has left is *refused* rather than restarting it — restarting would re-issue numbers documents already hold.

`SequenceScope::DepartmentYear` cannot honour it. Its bucket key is `{department}:{year}`, so two departments are two buckets that are live *at the same time* — and one row holds one. `allocate_in` decides what to do when the key it computed differs from the key stored, and `is_earlier_bucket` deliberately answers `false` for two different departments:

> A `DEPARTMENT_YEAR` key is prefixed by the department, so two different departments never compare as earlier or later — which is right, they are parallel sequences and neither has passed the other.

The reasoning is correct about sequences and wrong about storage. "Not earlier" falls through to the branch that restarts the counter at 1, so every allocation that changes department resets the bucket. Probe 1 allocated for department A, then B, then A, then B, against a rule whose template carries `{department}`:

```
PROBE1 a1=PR-…d66a-2026-000001 b1=PR-…79f2-2026-000001
       a2=PR-…d66a-2026-000001 b2=PR-…79f2-2026-000001
```

`a1 == a2` and `b1 == b2`. It is not that the second department disturbs the first — it is that **neither department's counter ever advances past 1** once both are in use, which is the normal case for the scope. In Sprint 9 the consequence is `uq_documents_tenant_id_document_number` refusing the second document of a department at submit, after the work is done: the exact failure `numbering_repository.rs` says its `FOR UPDATE` exists to prevent, arriving by another door.

**`two_departments_run_separate_sequences` passes while this happens**, and it is worth reading why. It allocates A then B, asserts both end `000001`, asserts they differ, and stops:

> the second department starts its own sequence rather than continuing the first's

That assertion is true and is the defect. "Starting its own sequence" is implemented by overwriting the only bucket the row has. A third allocation is what separates the two readings, and the test does not make one. This is record 03's F7 shape — a test that reads as thorough and stops one step before the property it names.

The fix is not in `is_earlier_bucket`. One bucket per rule and a multi-bucket scope are incompatible, so either `DEPARTMENT_YEAR` leaves the `CHECK` constraint or the counter moves to a row per bucket. Both are migrations, which is why this is `Must` and why it is release-blocking for `v0.4.0` rather than a Sprint 9 detail.

### F2 — a multi-argument `sum` is a silent zero on both sides

`evaluator.rs`'s header states the property the whole of D-10 rests on:

> **An unknown operator is an error, never a passthrough.** … A mistyped `sum` therefore turned a 42-rupiah invoice line into 0 with nothing logged and nothing refused.

That property holds for an unknown operator. It does not hold for `sum` itself. Probe 2 ran five shapes the parity corpus does not carry, on both engines:

| Expression | Data | Backend | Frontend |
|---|---|---|---|
| `{"sum":[{"var":"x"}]}` | `{"x":[1,"2",true,null,3]}` | `4` | `4` |
| `{"sum":[{"var":"x"}]}` | `{"x":7}` | `0` | `0` |
| `{"sum":[{"var":"a"},{"var":"b"}]}` | `{"a":1,"b":2}` | **`0`** | **`0`** |
| `{"sum":[{"var":"nope"}]}` | `{}` | `0` | `0` |
| `{"sum":[{"var":"x"}]}` | `{"x":[[1,2],3]}` | `3` | `3` |

The two agree on all five, which is D-10 working exactly as designed. The third row is the finding: `{"sum": [a, b]}` is the natural mistake, because `+` — the operator beside it in the registry, in the same allow-list, with the same bracket syntax — *does* take a list of operands. A form author who writes it gets `0`. The meta-schema accepts it (`jsonLogic` accepts any single-key object and defers arity), `domain/jfss.rs` accepts it (`sum` is in `CALCULATE_OPERATORS`), the client renders `0`, and JFSS S8.1's server-side re-evaluation confirms `0`. **Parity is what makes it invisible**: the Tamper-Proof Pattern catches a client that disagrees with the server, and here they agree.

The Calculation Rule Registry §3.2 specifies `{ "sum": [array] }` and the empty-array answer, and is silent on every other shape — so this is a specification gap first and an implementation behaviour second, which is why the issue asks for a decision rather than naming a fix.

**The corpus carries one of the three edge cases both implementations say they must agree on.** Each side's comment reads: "the two implementations have to agree on the same three edge cases: a non-array argument, an empty array, and non-numeric members." `parity/cases.json` has `sum`, `sum-empty`, `invoice-pattern` and `datagrid-typing-race`. Only the empty array is in it. The two agree today — Probe 2 measured that — and nothing in CI would notice if one of them stopped, because `sum` is the one part of the evaluator that is genuinely two implementations rather than one engine.

### F3 — the reset request is timeable

`reset.rs`'s header states the security property:

> **Account enumeration.** `request_reset` answers identically whether the address belongs to an account or not — same status, same body, and **no branch a caller can time**. Everything that could differ (no such user, an inactive one, a send that failed) happens **after the response is already decided**.

The response is *decided* before the send, and it is not *sent* until after it. `request_reset` awaits `state.mailer.send(...)`, and `Mailer::Smtp::send` awaits `transport.send(message)` — a full SMTP transaction — before returning; only then does `request_reset` return `Ok(())` and the 202 get written. The two paths are:

- **unknown identifier** — one `SELECT`, return;
- **known, active, with an address, outside the cooldown** — three `SELECT`s, an `INSERT`, **a complete SMTP round trip**, and an audit `INSERT`.

An SMTP round trip against a relay is tens to hundreds of milliseconds and is seconds against one that is slow or blackholing, against a path whose fast branch is a single indexed lookup. The endpoint is also deliberately not rate-limited — correctly, for the reason `handlers::routes` gives about refunding a brute-force's failure budget — so nothing bounds how many addresses a caller may time.

The cooldown narrows the oracle rather than closing it: inside `RESEND_COOLDOWN_SECONDS` a *known* account also returns fast, so a caller gets one clean measurement per account per minute. That is an enumeration rate, not an enumeration defence.

Not reproduced by measurement. `Mailer` is an enum rather than a trait, so a slow transport cannot be injected without changing the code under test, and the captured mailer used by the suite makes the send free — which is the reason the suite cannot see this. The finding rests on the call graph, and the issue asks for the measurement against mailpit as its first acceptance criterion.

### F4 — the audit hash cannot tell absent from empty

`chain_hash` length-prefixes every field, and the comment on the helper that renders an absent one says what the prefix buys:

> An absent field is zero bytes, which the length prefix keeps distinct from a present-but-empty one.

It does not. `optional(None)` is `&[]` and `optional(Some(""))` is also `&[]`; both are hashed as a length of 0 followed by nothing. Probe 3 hashed four entries differing only in `ip_address` and `reason`:

```
PROBE3 ip absent=sha256:cb22992945740aa4562d89805866c88d76e4fb613e3345777e090031952d0680
PROBE3 ip empty =sha256:cb22992945740aa4562d89805866c88d76e4fb613e3345777e090031952d0680
PROBE3 reason absent=sha256:cb2299…0680
PROBE3 reason empty =sha256:cb2299…0680
```

All four identical. A row whose `ip_address` is `NULL` and the same row with `''` verify against the same chain, so that column can be rewritten either way without breaking anything downstream — in a chain whose whole purpose (FR-AUD-003) is to make a rewrite detectable.

The claim the comment makes *about payloads* is true and is the one D-20 was written for: `None` hashes as zero bytes and `Some(Value::Null)` canonicalises to the four bytes `null`, so those two are distinct. The comment generalised a true statement about payloads into a false one about every optional field, in the change that existed to stop the chain from claiming coverage it did not have. The scope is narrow — `ip_address` and `reason` are the only `Option<&str>` fields, and nothing in the tree writes `Some("")` today — which is what keeps this Medium rather than High. It is still cheap to fix and free to fix *now*, for exactly the reason D-20 gives about breaking the format only once.

## 4. What was checked and found sound

Recorded because an absence of findings only means something with a boundary around it.

- **The OpenAPI gate is closed for good, not for this sprint.** All thirty-three route handlers Sprint 7 added reach `paths(...)`, and `every_annotated_route_reaches_the_document` now *reads the source* in both directions rather than naming routes. That is record 03's F6 fixed at the class rather than at the instance, and it is the first Definition-of-Done clause in the project asserted by a test that discovers its own subjects.
- **Tenant scoping is complete in the source across all three new modules** — and F7 is about whether it is *held*. Every statement in `rad/repository/`, `document_type/` and `organization/` carries a `tenant_id` predicate except two, and both omissions are right: `numbering_repository::advance` writes by an id read under `FOR UPDATE` in the same transaction, and the `tenants` statements act on the partition itself, guarded by `require_tenant_administrator` instead. This was checked statement by statement, not sampled — #108 was a join in a module whose doc promised otherwise. What the campaign then showed is that eight of those predicates are exercised by nothing, so "complete" here means present and read, not tested.
- **The department hierarchy applies all three of last sprint's lessons.** A tenant-wide advisory lock in the caller's transaction (#133), an ancestor walk that *reports* truncation instead of returning a short answer (#134), and both references re-read inside the transaction (#118/#137). `lock_department_hierarchy`'s comment carries the four-department counter-example that rules out the narrower lock. This is the first module in the project to get a hierarchy right on the first attempt.
- **Publication immutability holds, at the layer that is tested.** `a_published_revision_cannot_be_edited` and `publishing_twice_is_refused` are real, and the service check they exercise is correct. The statement-level `AND status = 'DRAFT'` on `update_draft` and `publish` is the second layer, for a publish landing between the read and the write, and `update_form` turns its zero-row result into a refusal by re-reading — all of which was read and none of which is exercised (F7). The design is right; the race it is designed for has never been run.
- **JFSS operator governance reaches every container the meta-schema defines.** The schema puts a `components` array in exactly five places — the root, both branches of the component `allOf`, `columnSlot` and `tabSlot` — and `walk_components` visits all five. `jsonLogic` is referenced from exactly three places and all three are covered; `advancedRule` carries a named rule and no expression, so it opens no ungoverned tier. The allow-list is a real boundary on an engine whose native surface is far wider than the registry approves.
- **Every `sum` shape the corpus does not carry still agrees across the two engines** — Probe 2's five rows, identical on both. F2 is about what the gate would not catch, not about a divergence that exists.
- **A new tenant's administrator role is built from the catalogue rather than from a list.** `permission_ids_excluding_prefix` reads every permission except the withheld family, so `rad:*` and `document-type:*` reached tenants provisioned after the migrations that seeded them, and the next module's permissions will too without anyone remembering.
- **The evaluator's known gap is documented where it is, measured, and identical on both sides.** `10.5 / 0` normalises to `0`; `10 / 0`, `0 / 0` and `10 % 0` throw on both builds. Pinned by tests on both sides, so a version bump that changes it is loud. That is a parity-preserving gap, which is a different thing from a divergence, and the code says so.
- **Numbering's concurrency is right where it was designed to be right, and it is tested there.** Removing `FOR UPDATE` from `lock_active_rule` turns `no_two_concurrent_allocations_take_the_same_number` red — over twenty-four concurrent allocations against a pool of five, which is the concurrency #118 taught this project a harness has to actually reach. `allocate_in` runs in the caller's transaction so a gapless rule rolls back with it, and `allocate` dispatches on the stored policy rather than assuming one. The lock is the one thing in this module that a mutation could not get past.
- **The cross-tenant read boundary holds** — Probe 4, 404 on another tenant's user with the permission held. Untested until this pass; see F5.
- **The two carry-overs did what the plan said they were for.** `departments` has a surface that populates the table `master_data/repository/role.rs` validates against, and `password_reset_tokens` — in the tree unread since `0006` — now has a reader.

## 6. What this changes about how the project verifies

**A scope enum and a storage shape are a design pair, and nothing holds them together.** F1 is not a coding mistake. `is_earlier_bucket` is correct about sequences, `allocate_in` is correct about locking, and `two_departments_run_separate_sequences` asserts what its author believed. The mistake is that a four-valued `CHECK` constraint and a one-row-one-bucket table were specified in the same section of the same document and never compared. The rule that would have caught it is not about tests: **an enum whose values imply different storage cardinalities names, per value, how many rows it needs** — and §6.3 states the cardinality it has, in bold, one paragraph away from the `CHECK` that violates it.

**A test that stops at the first half of a property asserts the half that is true.** Record 03's F7 was about statements no test reaches. This is the sharper version: the statement is reached, the test passes, and the assertion is a true sentence about a broken system. Both `two_departments_run_separate_sequences` (F1) and the quarantined body in F5 have it. What separates them from a real test is a *third* step — allocate for A again, give the caller the permission so the refusal is about tenancy — and in both cases the third step is one line. **A test over a shared resource makes at least three moves, because two moves cannot distinguish "separate" from "overwritten".**

**Parity hides a class of defect as effectively as it catches another.** D-10 bought the property that the server and the client compute the same thing, and F2 is what that costs: when both are wrong in the same way, the Tamper-Proof Pattern re-evaluates and *confirms*. The corpus is where a shared-wrongness defect would be caught, and it is built from claims the registry makes — so it covers what the registry specifies and is silent exactly where the registry is silent. **A parity corpus needs cases the specification does not mention**, because the specification's gaps are where the two implementations agree by accident rather than by design.

**A retired decision leaves debris, and nothing sweeps for it.** D-18 superseded D-7 and its record is thorough — it lists eleven documents and three issues it was applied to. It did not list the one `#[ignore]` D-7 was holding down, because nothing enumerates what a decision is load-bearing for. F5 is small; the rule is not. **A decision that supersedes another names what the superseded one was holding, and a superseding decision's "Applied to" list includes the code that cited the old decision by name** — which is greppable, and was not grepped.

**Defence in depth is a claim, and this project does not test claims of that kind at all.** Sixteen of F7's twenty greens are second layers behind a first layer that works. Every one was written deliberately — `update_draft`'s statement predicate has a paragraph of module documentation explaining why it is in the statement and not only in the service — and not one of them has ever been exercised, because exercising a second layer means disabling the first, and nothing in the suite can. The result is that the project's most careful code is its least verified code, which is an inversion worth naming. **A predicate written as a second line of defence needs a test that removes the first**, and where that cannot be arranged the module comment should say the layer is unverified rather than say why it exists.

**The strongest claims are still the best targets.** Record 03 closed with that sentence and this pass is the second data point. F1, F3 and F4 were each found by taking one sentence of module documentation as a hypothesis and trying to break it: "one bucket per rule", "no branch a caller can time", "the length prefix keeps absent distinct from present-but-empty". All three were written by an author who had just thought hard about the thing, which is what makes them precise enough to test and what makes nobody retest them.

## 7. Disposition

All six findings are filed. **F1 is release-blocking for `v0.4.0`** and needs a migration, so it belongs in Sprint 8 rather than Sprint 9 — the schema it changes is the one Sprint 9's submit path builds on, and a numbering change after that path exists is a change with a consumer.

F2 raises a decision (what a malformed `sum` should do) rather than naming a fix, and the decision is Sprint 8's, because Sprint 8 item 4 is the server-side re-evaluation that would confirm the zero.

F3, F4, F5 and F6 are `Should` and fit Sprint 8's verification allowance. F4 is worth taking early for D-20's own reason: the hash format is free to change exactly once more.

**F7 is `Must` and is not a list of tests to write.** Four of its twenty need tests now, because nothing else holds their behaviour. The other sixteen need the rule the Sprint 6 retrospective asked for and did not get — and the choice in front of the product owner is whether to make that rule checkable by a reviewer or retire it and put a mutation campaign per sprint in its place. A third consecutive sprint reporting this finding is the outcome to avoid, and the [Sprint 7 retrospective](../retrospectives/05.%20Sprint%207%20Retrospective.md) says so as its first action.
